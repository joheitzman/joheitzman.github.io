About

Coral eco-physiology · Coral-algae interactions · Coral disease & microbes · Ocean acidification & warming

I am a marine ecologist studying how climate change, especially ocean warming and acidification, reshapes coral ecosystems. My research focuses on coral eco-physiology, the microbial communities of corals and algae, and the interactions between the two: from algae-driven coral disease outbreaks, to turf algae that dissolve coral skeletons at natural CO2 seeps, to the physiological traits that help some corals cope with acidification.

I am an Assistant Professor at the Takehara Fisheries Research Station, Seto Inland Sea Carbon-neutral Research Center, Hiroshima University. Before this I was a post-doctoral researcher and then a JSPS Postdoctoral Fellow at the same center (Prof. Shigeki Wada), studying how ocean acidification affects the carbon sequestration capacity (blue carbon) of coastal ecosystems. As part of this work I took part in the 2025 TARA-JAMBIO Blue Carbon expedition, surveying coastal algae across temperate Japan (read about it here).

I received my Ph.D. from the University of Tsukuba (Shimoda Marine Research Center) under Dr. Sylvain Agostini, studying coral-algae interactions in the Anthropocene, and was a post-doctoral researcher at Arizona State University (Dr. Liza Roger), working on nitro-oxidative stress in the coral-dinoflagellate symbiosis.

Research & Publications

My research asks how ocean warming and acidification change corals and the communities around them. I study coral eco-physiology, from mitochondrial energy production to calcification and skeletal dissolution; the microbial communities of corals, algae and coral disease; and how competition between corals and algae shifts as conditions change. I combine SCUBA field surveys, natural CO2 seeps as windows into a future ocean, aquarium experiments and lab techniques from microsensors to microbiome sequencing. With collaborators I have also worked on the collapse of kelp forests under marine heatwaves and on how ocean acidification affects the body size of marine animals.

Turf algae dissolve coral skeletons under ocean acidification

Ocean acidification · Shikine Island CO2 seeps · Global Change Biology Communications 2026

As CO2 rises, turf algae gain the upper hand over corals. We grew standardised coral-turf "microcolonies" of two corals, Acropora solitaryensis and Porites heronensis, in aquaria and at the volcanic CO2 seeps off Shikine Island. Turf algae won the competition under high CO2, and their activity acidified the thin layer of water over the exposed coral skeleton, so the skeleton passively dissolved. As turf spreads, reefs could tip towards net dissolution.

Abstract

Ocean acidification (OA) is expected to lower seawater pH and aragonite saturation (Ωarag), altering reef carbonate budgets through combined effects on calcification, dissolution, and species interactions. However, OA may benefit low-profile algal turfs, comprised of several epilithic species, that tend to settle onto exposed coral skeletons following disturbance events. Despite the future predicted prevalence of turf algae across ecosystems, their contribution to future reef ecology and carbonate budget remains poorly understood. Coral-turf algae competition and carbonate dynamics under future high pCO2 conditions were investigated by using standardized coral-turf algae microcolonies representative of future community-level interactions. Experiments were conducted using two coral species, Acropora solitaryensis and Porites heronensis, in both controlled aquaria and a natural CO2 seep environment in a marginal coral community. Turf algae were competitively favored under high pCO2 conditions, highlighting the importance of biotic interactions in shaping future reef communities. The local pH microenvironment under the turf, resulting from associated biological activity, combined with OA, promoted the chemical passive dissolution of the coral carbonate skeleton. Turf proliferation under climate change and OA will influence carbonate budgets through a combination of biotic and abiotic mechanisms, contributing to the shift of reefs towards a state of net dissolution.

Heitzman, J.M., Iijima, L., Mitushasi, G., Spatafora, D., Wada, S., Harvey, B.P., Kurihara, H., Agostini, S. (2026). Global Change Biology Communications 1(3), e70043. doi:10.1002/gcb4.70043

Seasonal coral-algae interactions drive White Mat Syndrome outbreaks

Coral disease · Shimoda · Science of The Total Environment 2023

In summer, the red alga Gelidium elegans starts to break down and becomes a breeding ground for the White Mat Syndrome microbial mat. Infection experiments showed that the mat spreads to neighbouring Porites corals by direct contact and through drifting fragments, and pointed to a consortium of sulfur-cycling bacteria as the likely pathogens.

Abstract

Ocean warming drives not only the increase of known coral disease prevalence but facilitates the emergence of new undescribed ones too. As climate change is restructuring coral ecosystems, novel biological interactions could lead to an increase in coral disease in both tropical and marginal coral communities. White Mat Syndrome (WMS) represents one such emerging coral disease, with outbreaks associated with high algal interactions and seasonal summer temperatures. However, the mechanisms behind its pathogenesis, modes of transmission and causative pathogens remain to be identified. Ex situ infection experiments pairing the coral Porites heronensis together with local potential contributory factors show that the macroalga Gelidium elegans hosts and proliferates the WMS microbial mat. This pathogenic consortium then infects adjacent corals, leading to their mortality. WMS was also observed to transmit following the fragmentation of the microbial mat, which was able to infect healthy corals. Sulfur-cycling bacteria (i.e., Beggiatoa, Desulfobacter sp., Arcobacteraceae species) and the freeliving spirochete Oceanospirochaeta sediminicola were found consistently in both WMS and G. elegans consortia, suggesting they are putative pathogens of WMS. The predicted functional roles of these pathogenic consortia showed degradative processes, hinting that tissue lyses could drive mat formation and spread. Coral-algae interactions will rise due to ongoing ocean warming and coral ecosystem degradation, likely promoting the virulence and prevalence of algal-driven coral diseases.

Heitzman, J.M., Mitushasi, G., Spatafora, D., Agostini, S. (2023). Science of The Total Environment 900, 166379. doi:10.1016/j.scitotenv.2023.166379

A recurrent coral disease outbreak in a warm-temperate coral community

Coral disease · Shimoda · Marine Pollution Bulletin 2022

The first description of a coral disease outbreak in warm-temperate waters. Since 2014, a white bacterial mat dominated by the sulfide-oxidising Thiothrix has been killing Porites heronensis colonies off Shimoda after hot summers, with the red alga Gelidium elegans acting as a reservoir. Emerging diseases like this could undermine the idea of high-latitude reefs as climate refuges.

Abstract

Coral diseases contribute to the rapid degradation of coral reefs on a global scale. Although widespread in tropical and subtropical reefs, disease outbreaks have not been described in warm temperate areas. Here, we report the outbreak of a new coral disease in a warm temperate marginal coral community in Japan. Outbreaks of the disease have been observed during the summer and autumn months since 2014. It affects the coral species Porites heronensis and was tentatively named "White Mat Syndrome" (WMS) as it consists of a white microbial mat dominated by Thiothrix sp., a sulfide oxidizing bacteria. Outbreaks followed high seasonal temperatures and were associated with the macroalga Gelidium elegans, which acts as a pathogen reservoir. With ocean warming and the anticipated increase in novel coral-algae interactions as some coral species shift poleward, WMS and emerging diseases could hinder the role of temperate areas as a future coral refuge.

Heitzman, J.M., Caputo, N., Yang, S.-Y., Harvey, B.P., Agostini, S. (2022). Marine Pollution Bulletin 182, 113954. doi:10.1016/j.marpolbul.2022.113954

Cellular stress in the coral symbiosis Arizona State University, 2024

In Dr. Liza Roger's Marine Biochem Lab (School of Molecular Sciences and School of Ocean Futures), I went from the reef down to the single cell to look at what triggers coral bleaching. Under heat stress, reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS/RNS, or free radicals) build up inside coral cells, and the coral ends up expelling the algae that live inside it. Coral studies usually measure the total oxidative stress of a whole colony, and mostly look only at hydrogen peroxide, so which free radicals actually drive bleaching is still unclear.

Biomedical researchers already have a large toolkit of assays and fluorescent probes for measuring specific free radicals in live cells, so I tested whether it works in corals too. I dissociated live cells from the coral Pocillopora acuta, checked their viability with nuclear stains, and then ran a panel of assays and probes for hydrogen peroxide, superoxide, nitric oxide and peroxynitrite, in the cytosol, the mitochondria and the space outside the cell. Each was validated against positive and negative controls before comparing cells at 25 °C and 30 °C.

Presented as Investigating extra- and intracellular free radical dynamics in vitro in Pocillopora acuta cells, ASU Ocean Futures Symposium 2024 (J.M. Heitzman, S. Chowdury, A. Padula, D. Domaille, D. Wangpraseurt, H. Putnam & L. Roger).

Blue carbon in a high-CO2 ocean Hiroshima University, 2025 – present

Seaweed and seagrass beds capture and store carbon. At Hiroshima University I study how ocean acidification and warming change these coastal ecosystems and their capacity to sequester carbon, from the Seto Inland Sea to coasts across Japan, including the TARA-JAMBIO Blue Carbon surveys. Lab

We study how a changing ocean rewires the interactions that hold coastal ecosystems together. As seawater warms and absorbs more CO2, the balance between corals, algae and microbes shifts, with knock-on effects for reef growth, disease and the resilience of coastal ecosystems. We combine fieldwork, natural CO2 seeps and controlled experiments to work out the mechanisms behind these shifts and what they mean for the future of coastal seas.

What we do

Field monitoring

Long-term SCUBA monitoring of coral and algal communities, built on years of fieldwork at Shimoda and the natural CO2 seeps of Shikine Island, nationwide coastal surveys with the TARA-JAMBIO Blue Carbon expedition (Kagawa, Niigata, Hokkaido and Miyagi), and work within the ICONA network of natural CO2 analogue sites. Our home base is now the Seto Inland Sea.

Microbial ecology

Characterising the microbial communities of corals, turf algae and coral disease mats using 16S amplicon sequencing (QIIME2) and functional prediction (PICRUSt2), to understand how microbes mediate coral-algae interactions, disease outbreaks and ecosystem degradation.

Coral eco-physiology

Measuring how corals and their symbionts respond to warming and acidification: calcification, photosynthesis and respiration, microsensor profiling of the coral surface, seawater carbonate chemistry, stable isotopes, and cell-level imaging with confocal microscopy.

Molecular responses to climate change

Linking what we see in the field and in physiology to changes in gene content and gene expression, using genomic and transcriptomic approaches to identify how corals, algae and their microbes respond and adapt to a changing ocean.
