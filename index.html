Joshua M. Heitzman, Ph.D.

coral eco-physiology | coral disease dynamics | ocean acidification | blue carbon

I am a marine ecologist interested in how environmental change, especially ocean acidification and ocean warming, reshapes biological interactions in coastal ecosystems, and what those shifts mean for corals, algae and the carbon these ecosystems store.

I am an Assistant Professor at the Takehara Fisheries Research Station, Seto Inland Sea Carbon-neutral Research Center, Hiroshima University. Before this I was a post-doctoral researcher and then a JSPS Postdoctoral Fellow at the same center (Prof. Shigeki Wada), studying how ocean acidification affects the carbon sequestration capacity (blue carbon) of coastal ecosystems. As part of this work I took part in the 2025 TARA-JAMBIO Blue Carbon expedition, surveying coastal algae across temperate Japan (read about it here).

I received my Ph.D. from the University of Tsukuba (Shimoda Marine Research Center) under Dr. Sylvain Agostini, studying coral-algae interactions in the Anthropocene, and was a post-doctoral researcher at Arizona State University (Dr. Liza Roger), working on nitro-oxidative stress in the coral-dinoflagellate symbiosis.

Research

My work combines field surveys (SCUBA), natural CO2 seep sites, aquarium experiments and lab techniques from microsensors to microbiome sequencing. It follows a few connected themes:

Coral-algae interactions under ocean acidification

As CO2 rises, turf algae gain the upper hand over corals. Using laboratory experiments and transplants at volcanic CO2 seeps off Shikine Island, I study how algae alter the chemistry next to coral tissue, and how this can turn coral skeleton from something that is built into something that is eroded.

Coral disease in marginal, warm-temperate communities

I described White Mat Syndrome, a recurring coral disease in temperate Japan linked to summer temperatures and the red alga Gelidium elegans, which acts as a reservoir for the pathogenic microbial mat. This work asks whether high-latitude coral communities can really act as climate refuges.

Blue carbon in a high-CO2 ocean

Seaweed and seagrass beds capture and store carbon. At Hiroshima University I am investigating how ocean acidification changes these coastal ecosystems and their capacity to sequester carbon, from the Seto Inland Sea to the wider Japanese coastline.

Cellular stress in the coral symbiosis

At the cellular scale, I have used cell dissociation, confocal microscopy and fluorescent probes to explore how nitro-oxidative stress affects the partnership between corals and their dinoflagellate symbionts.

    Lab

    We study how a changing ocean rewires the interactions that hold coastal ecosystems together. As seawater warms and absorbs more CO2, the balance between corals, algae and microbes shifts, with knock-on effects for reef growth, disease and the carbon these habitats store. We combine fieldwork, natural CO2 seeps and controlled experiments to work out the mechanisms behind these shifts and what they mean for the future of coastal seas.

    What we do

    Field monitoring

    Long-term SCUBA monitoring of coral and algal communities, built on years of fieldwork at Shimoda and the natural CO2 seeps of Shikine Island, nationwide coastal surveys with the TARA-JAMBIO Blue Carbon expedition (Kagawa, Niigata, Hokkaido and Miyagi), and work within the ICONA network of natural CO2 analogue sites. Our home base is now the Seto Inland Sea.

    Microbial ecology

    Characterising the microbial communities of corals, turf algae and coral disease mats using 16S amplicon sequencing (QIIME2) and functional prediction (PICRUSt2), to understand how microbes mediate coral-algae interactions, disease outbreaks and ecosystem degradation.

    Coral eco-physiology

    Measuring how corals and their symbionts respond to warming and acidification: calcification, photosynthesis and respiration, microsensor profiling of the coral surface, seawater carbonate chemistry, stable isotopes, and cell-level imaging with confocal microscopy.

    Molecular responses to climate change

    Linking what we see in the field and in physiology to changes in gene content and gene expression, using genomic and transcriptomic approaches to identify how corals, algae and their microbes respond and adapt to a changing ocean.

    Publications

    Featured work (click an image to read the paper)

    Turf algae drives coral bioerosion under high CO2
    Turf algal prevalence will increase in coral ecosystems under ocean acidification yet their contribution towards the ongoing and projected degradation of reefs is often overlooked. Turf algal settlement was induced on exposed coral skeleton adjacent to live coral tissue to investigate coral-turf algal interactions through a combination of laboratory and field transplantation (shallow volcanic CO2 seep) experiments across two temperature regimes. Here, we show that turf algae are competitively favored over corals under high pCO2 conditions. Turf algae-associated biological activity locally acidified the microenvironment overlying the exposed coral skeleton, leading to its bioerosion. Increases in coral-turf algal interactions could shift coral ecosystems towards net dissolution and should be integrated into global accretion models when considering future carbonate budgets under climate change.
    Seasonal coral-algae interactions drive White Mat Syndrome coral disease outbreaks
    Ocean warming drives not only the increase of known coral disease prevalence but facilitates the emergence of new undescribed ones too. As climate change is restructuring coral ecosystems, novel biological interactions could lead to an increase in coral disease in both tropical and marginal coral communities. White Mat Syndrome (WMS) represents one such emerging coral disease, with outbreaks associated with high algal interactions and seasonal summer temperatures. However, the mechanisms behind its pathogenesis, modes of transmission and causative pathogens remain to be identified. Ex situ infection experiments pairing the coral Porites heronensis together with local potential contributory factors show that the macroalga Gelidium elegans hosts and proliferates the WMS microbial mat. This pathogenic consortium then infects adjacent corals, leading to their mortality. WMS was also observed to transmit following the fragmentation of the microbial mat, which was able to infect healthy corals. Sulfur-cycling bacteria (i.e., Beggiatoa, Desulfobacter sp., Arcobacteraceae species) and the freeliving spirochete Oceanospirochaeta sediminicola were found consistently in both WMS and G. elegans consortia, suggesting they are putative pathogens of WMS. The predicted functional roles of these pathogenic consortia showed degradative processes, hinting that tissue lyses could drive mat formation and spread. Coral-algae interactions will rise due to ongoing ocean warming and coral ecosystem degradation, likely promoting the virulence and prevalence of algal-driven coral diseases.
    Recurrent disease outbreak in a warm temperate marginal coral community
    Coral diseases contribute to the rapid degradation of coral reefs on a global scale. Although widespread in tropical and subtropical reefs, disease outbreaks have not been described in warm temperate areas. Here, we report the outbreak of a new coral disease in a warm temperate marginal coral community in Japan. Outbreaks of the disease have been observed during the summer and autumn months since 2014. It affects the coral species Porites heronensis and was tentatively named "White Mat Syndrome" (WMS) as it consists of a white microbial mat dominated by Thiothrix sp., a sulfide oxidizing bacteria. Outbreaks followed high seasonal temperatures and were associated with the macroalga Gelidium elegans, which acts as a pathogen reservoir. With ocean warming and the anticipated increase in novel coral-algae interactions as some coral species shift poleward, WMS and emerging diseases could hinder the role of temperate areas as a future coral refuge.
